# Jeremy Ehly | Website Voice Style Guide

Updated July 29, 2026.

This guide governs authored copy across the website. It draws from my teaching and service statements, syllabus introductions, assignment rationales, reflections on teaching, and the language I use when describing my work.

## Voice In One Sentence

My voice is purposeful, candid, humane, and selectively playful. I write as a practicing designer working alongside students: I begin with why, name the stakes plainly, set a high standard, and leave room for curiosity, experimentation, and humor.

## Working Voice Statement

I write as a designer and teacher working alongside my students. I begin with why, name the stakes plainly, and connect ideas to what people actually make, test, and learn. My tone is direct, optimistic, and demanding without becoming formal or distant. I use humor as a small release valve, never as a substitute for substance. I am personal when experience earns it, candid about uncertainty, and clear that curiosity, effort, judgment, and care matter more than polish alone.

## Core Qualities

### Purposeful

I explain why something matters before describing how it works. Values should be connected to a course decision, student action, built consequence, or lesson learned.

### Direct

I use active language and make claims plainly. I do not hide my point behind institutional phrasing, academic abstraction, or a long preamble.

### Humane

I write with respect for students as adults, collaborators, and developing designers. Care appears through listening, clear expectations, honest reflection, and attention to what students need to grow.

### Selectively Playful

Humor is part of my teaching, but it is not a performance. It works best as a specific aside or an unexpected detail attached to real work. Ducks, dad jokes, and Comic Sans in extenuating circumstances belong. Manufactured punchlines and clever slogans do not.

### Optimistic, But Realistic

I make room for ambition while acknowledging constraints, uncertainty, failure, workload, and consequences. Optimism should feel practiced rather than promotional.

## Point Of View

All authored website copy should sound first-person and direct.

+ Use **I** for my intentions, decisions, observations, responsibilities, and lessons learned.
+ Use **we** only for experiences genuinely shared with students, collaborators, or a project team.
+ Use **students** when preserving their agency and documenting what they researched, made, tested, or learned.
+ Use **you** sparingly. Invite the reader when useful, but do not turn the portfolio into a lecture or sales pitch.

Do not use a vague **we** to blur responsibility. If I created a course, led a project, or made a decision, say **I**. When a class built something collectively, say **we** and credit the team.

**Refinement, confirmed July 29, 2026:** applied literally, the rule above can still read as forceful when "I" describes an action done to students, for example "I built Luminaire Studio" or "I sent students to Denver Botanic Gardens." Prefer **we** for the shared exploration or process itself ("We started our design exploration looking for inspiration from the environments of Denver Botanic Gardens, built or natural, architecture or dandelions. Those observations and inspirations became the conceptual guides for their luminaire design process."), and reserve **I** for genuine feeling or plain biographical fact ("I joined the Department of Environmental Design at the University of Colorado Boulder in 2010," "I feel it is a great honor and privilege to collaborate with such talented and principled students. I hold our work together in the highest esteem."). Always establish inspiration and intention before form or technical requirements.

## How I Build A Paragraph

1. Begin with the reason, tension, or question.
2. Explain what I designed, asked, changed, or learned.
3. Show what students or collaborators did in response.
4. End with a concrete consequence, insight, or honest reflection.

Paragraphs should generally be two to four sentences. Vary sentence length, but keep the main claim easy to find. Use active verbs and specific nouns.

## Characteristic Moves

These patterns already occur naturally in my writing and can be used deliberately:

+ Start with why.
+ Pair high expectations with trust and support.
+ Connect thinking to making.
+ Use a clear contrast: effort over talent, process over outcome, optimism tempered by realism, or technology supporting rather than replacing judgment.
+ Admit uncertainty when it is honest and useful.
+ Move from a specific classroom or project decision to its broader implication.
+ Place a brief human aside after the substance is established.

Do not repeat the same contrast or formula on every page. The voice should remain recognizable without making every course sound identical.

## Language To Favor

Use concrete verbs:

+ ask
+ build
+ choose
+ connect
+ draw
+ listen
+ make
+ notice
+ question
+ research
+ revise
+ test
+ understand

Use these recurring ideas when the evidence supports them:

+ agency
+ care
+ consequence
+ curiosity
+ empathy
+ effort
+ experimentation
+ judgment
+ purpose
+ resilience
+ responsibility
+ rigor

## Language To Use Sparingly

These words reflect real values, but they become vague when stacked or repeated:

+ meaningful
+ thoughtful
+ intentional
+ critical
+ innovative
+ transformative
+ impactful
+ dynamic
+ authentic

Whenever one appears, ask whether a concrete action or result could make the claim instead.

## Language To Avoid

+ Corporate claims such as **leveraging synergies**, **driving innovation**, or **thought leadership**.
+ Institutional distance such as **the course foregrounds**, **this work positions**, or **learners are provided with**.
+ Unsupported superlatives such as **groundbreaking**, **world-class**, or **revolutionary**.
+ Promotional filler such as **at the intersection of**, **in today's rapidly changing world**, or **pushing the boundaries**.
+ Sentimentality that speaks for students or turns their experience into my personal redemption story.
+ Self-deprecation used to make expertise more acceptable.
+ Glibness that reduces an ethical, ecological, or educational question to a tagline.

## Humor

Humor should reveal how I teach, not become a persona I have to maintain.

Use humor when it:

+ lowers the pressure around learning a difficult skill
+ gives students permission to experiment
+ emerges from a real prompt, artifact, or classroom moment
+ adds one human beat to otherwise serious material

Do not use humor when it:

+ competes with student work
+ trivializes risk, ethics, identity, or ecological harm
+ sounds like social-media copy
+ depends on the reader already knowing me
+ appears simply because a page feels too serious

One light aside is usually enough.

## Humility And Authority

I do not need to minimize my role, but I should distinguish clearly between my contribution and the work of others.

+ State my responsibilities directly.
+ Credit students and collaborators accurately.
+ Let awards, outcomes, and evidence support claims of excellence.
+ Include what I learned or would reconsider when it adds genuine insight.
+ Avoid declaring myself innovative, compassionate, or influential. Show the decisions and results that allow readers to reach those conclusions.

## Voice By Site Area

### Homepage

Be personal, brief, and declarative. Establish my central concerns without attempting to summarize my career. Move quickly from values to evidence.

### AI Leadership

Sound curious, informed, and appropriately cautious. Be clear about both possibility and risk. Avoid technological hype and predictions presented as certainty.

### Teaching

Begin with what I wanted students to understand and why I structured the course as I did. Describe student work with specificity and respect. Reflections may include pride, uncertainty, adjustment, and lessons learned.

### Work

State my role, collaborators, constraints, and project status plainly. Explain the design proposition and my contribution without inflating either. Collective work should sound collective.

### About

Be personal without becoming confessional. Connect experience, values, and current direction. Detailed appointments and credentials belong in the resume unless they help explain the person or practice.

## Course Page Pattern

A course page should answer five questions in my voice:

1. Why did I create or frame this course in this way?
2. What did I ask students to investigate or make?
3. How did the structure support experimentation, judgment, and growth?
4. What does the selected work demonstrate?
5. What did the course teach me, or what am I still reconsidering?

Course descriptions should not read like catalog copy. Learning outcomes and technical details may remain concise and factual, but the surrounding narrative should explain my choices.

## Examples

### Institutional To Direct

Avoid:

> Generative Design with AI is an advanced seminar that foregrounds authorship, agency, and ethical responsibility.

Prefer:

> I created Generative Design with AI because learning the tools was only half the problem. I wanted students to ask what happens to authorship, judgment, and creative resilience when software begins participating in cognition.

### Abstract To Concrete

Avoid:

> The course creates meaningful opportunities for iterative and collaborative learning.

Prefer:

> I ask students to show unfinished work early, listen to one another, and revise before their ideas become too precious to change.

### Promotional To Evidenced

Avoid:

> My innovative design-build pedagogy gives students transformative real-world experience.

Prefer:

> I teach design-build because a drawing can conceal an unresolved decision; a building cannot. Students have to account for cost, weather, fabrication, clients, deadlines, and one another.

### Personal Without Becoming Confessional

Avoid:

> This project changed me forever and reminded me why I was born to teach.

Prefer:

> Watching students take responsibility for work that other people would actually use changed how I understood rigor. The deadline was no longer mine to enforce. It belonged to the project and the people waiting for it.

### Humor With A Job

Avoid:

> Learning Rhino does not have to be boring. Let the fun begin!

Prefer:

> I could have introduced NURBS modeling through a dry sequence of commands. Instead, I asked everyone to model a duck and give it a context. The resulting puns were terrible; the experimentation was excellent.

## Editing Checklist

Before publishing, ask:

1. Does this sound like I am speaking, or like an institution describing me?
2. Is the reason clear before the process becomes detailed?
3. Have I made my role clear without taking ownership of student or collaborative work?
4. Does each value claim have evidence nearby?
5. Could I replace a vague adjective with a specific action, object, decision, or result?
6. Is the tone confident without becoming self-congratulatory?
7. Is any humor connected to a real relationship, prompt, or experience?
8. Have I left room for complexity, uncertainty, or reflection where it matters?
9. Can I remove a sentence without losing meaning?
10. Would I say this aloud to a student, colleague, or prospective collaborator?

## Final Standard

The writing should feel like a thoughtful conversation with someone who cares about the work. It should be clear enough to trust, specific enough to remember, and human enough that the person behind the portfolio remains visible.
