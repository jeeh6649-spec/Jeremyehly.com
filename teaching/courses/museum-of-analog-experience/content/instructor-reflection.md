# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the Spring 2025 syllabus and course archive. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

I framed this capstone around a contradiction that felt increasingly relevant: we would use some of the most advanced digital tools available to design places where people could step away from digital life. I did not see that as hypocrisy. It created an opportunity to ask students to distinguish between technology as a deliberate instrument and technology as a condition that quietly controls attention.

The museum brief gave us a public framework for that question. A museum already asks people to pause, notice, move, gather, and interpret. By removing the assumption that its primary purpose was to display a conventional collection, students could reconsider the building itself as an instrument of experience. Programs emerged around making, performance, play, conversation, food, nature, silence, changing light, and the physical awareness of moving through space.

I wanted students to begin with a position rather than a shape. Interviews, discussions, site visits, and research into screen use helped them define whom the project served and what kind of disconnection or reconnection mattered. The point of view became a standard against which later decisions could be tested: Does this plan support the intended social experience? Does this material invite touch? Does the sequence alter pace and attention? Does the building offer something that a screen cannot?

At the same time, the studio embraced hybrid practice. Students moved among analog interventions, sketches, models, AI-assisted exploration, VR, digital modeling, and visualization. My goal was not to prescribe one correct workflow. It was to help each student understand what a tool contributed, where it introduced distance, and when another medium would produce a more useful form of knowledge.

The selected projects demonstrate three different architectural responses. One uses gradients of light, scale, sound, compression, and openness to recalibrate attention. Another turns circulation into a journey through creative work while allowing its copper facade to register change over time. A third creates a civic atrium that connects galleries, performance, making, food, and play behind a filtered public envelope.

Looking back, I am most interested in the way the studio joined critical inquiry to architectural resolution. The social premise could not remain a written argument; students had to carry it into plans, sections, structure, material, and atmosphere. That translation is where capstone teaching becomes meaningful. Students are no longer completing isolated exercises. They are learning to sustain an idea across the many decisions required to make architecture coherent.

The course also reinforced something I want to protect in future studios: technological fluency is most valuable when students have a clear human purpose against which to judge it. I want them to leave capable of working with emerging tools, but not dazzled into surrendering their agency. The measure of the process is not how advanced it appears. It is whether the resulting architecture helps people live, notice, and connect more fully.
