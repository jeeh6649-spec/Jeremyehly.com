# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the syllabus, assignment, student work, and course evaluations. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

The luminaire is a particularly effective first product-design challenge because it is immediately understandable and surprisingly complex. Students know what a lamp does, but resolving one requires them to coordinate structure, material, wiring, heat, diffusion, shadow, scale, atmosphere, fabrication, and use.

I structured this studio to give students experience with a complete design process in a compressed period. We began with precedent and digital modeling, moved into site observation and conceptual abstraction, and then shifted quickly into full-scale prototyping. The Denver Botanic Gardens gave the class a shared context while still allowing each student to identify a different source of meaning. The resulting projects range from botanical and geological references to questions of shelter, season, stillness, and the relationship between natural and constructed form.

The most important transition occurred when the work left the screen. A rendered object can appear resolved before its material logic has been tested. Full-scale fabrication exposed proportion, stability, light leakage, tolerances, wiring, surface quality, and the sequence of assembly. Students learned that craft is not decoration applied after design; it is one of the ways an idea becomes credible.

The course evaluations affirm the value of that hands-on progression, but they also identify a recurring challenge in eight-week studios: students wanted more time for fabrication and less time in the early precedent phase. I agree with that direction. A future iteration should preserve the conceptual and technical foundation while bringing physical experiments forward, allowing students to discover material constraints earlier and refine the final fixture through more than one full-scale cycle.

I am proud of how individual the outcomes became. The assignment had common constraints, yet the work does not read as a single formal exercise. Students developed their own relationships with light and carried those ideas through making, revision, and communication.
