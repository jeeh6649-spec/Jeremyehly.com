Capstone / Thesis

EPOD Capstone Studio

EPOD 4100|Selected teaching, 2016 to 2024|University of Colorado Boulder
A capstone should reveal what a student has learned and what they are ready to care about next.

Environmental Product Design Capstone gave students sustained time to develop work grounded in their own interests, emerging professional goals, and responsibility to others. Projects began with a problem arena rather than a predetermined object, moving through research, systems thinking, ideation, prototyping, critique, refinement, fabrication, and documentation toward a resolved public outcome.

The course balanced individual agency with collective support. Each student held primary responsibility for the direction and progress of their project while working within a studio culture of peer critique, shared technical knowledge, one-to-one desk reviews, professional mentorship, and clear milestones. Independence did not mean isolation.

The documented course model supported three paths: self-directed projects developed with an outside professional mentor, client-led work responding to a real partner's need, and values-led projects beginning from a personal cause and refined through design sprints. All three paths shared the same expectation: service to real people, professional readiness, and work resolved enough to stand on its own, across furniture, products, graphic design, packaging, publications, and communication systems.

Studio Framing
Freedom Within a Framework
Capstone is one of the rare moments in a student's education when the question can become genuinely personal. After years of responding to briefs, they are asked to decide what deserves sustained attention and what kind of designer they want to become. My role was to help students turn personal ambition into a project with clear stakes, disciplined progress, and a standard of completion they could defend.
We spent real time learning to love the problem before committing to a solution: researching beyond the surface, speaking with people who understood the issue through experience, and writing a problem statement that could guide difficult decisions later. Once a direction was established, milestones, desk critiques, peer reviews, mentor conversations, and repeated prototypes made progress visible, while full-scale prototypes exposed the questions that drawings could postpone: comfort, balance, tolerances, weight, assembly, and the reality of another person's body.
The range of work in this collection is one of the things I value most: furniture, gardening systems, safety equipment, play, publications, packaging, wearable objects, and tools for creative work. None of it shares a visual style, and it shouldn't. What I'm proudest of is watching students take themselves seriously as designers before graduation, finding a real opportunity, seeking expertise, making repeatedly, and presenting the result with confidence.

Frank Furniture responds to the waste generated by short-term living and frequently discarded furniture. Two CNC-cut modular components join without external fasteners or tools to form shelves, desks, chairs, tables, benches, and larger assemblies that can be disassembled, reconfigured, transported, or shared.
