# Environmental Product Design Capstone

**EPOD 4100 | Selected teaching, 2016-2024 | University of Colorado Boulder**

A capstone should reveal what a student has learned and what they are ready to care about next.

Environmental Product Design Capstone gave students sustained time to develop work grounded in their own interests, emerging professional goals, and responsibility to others. Projects began with a problem arena rather than a predetermined object. Through research, systems thinking, ideation, prototyping, critique, refinement, fabrication, and documentation, students carried an idea toward a resolved public outcome.

The course balanced individual agency with collective support. Each student held primary responsibility for the direction and progress of their project while working within a studio culture of peer critique, shared technical knowledge, one-to-one desk reviews, professional mentorship, and clear milestones. Independence did not mean isolation.

## Multiple Paths Through Capstone

The documented course model supported three approaches:

- **Self-directed:** Students proposed a critical project connected to a personal interest and worked with an outside professional mentor.
- **Client-led:** Students worked with a professional or community partner to research, propose, develop, and build a response to a real need.
- **Values-led:** Students began with a personal value, cause, passion, or user group, used short design sprints to explore multiple possibilities, and developed one direction into a finished product.

These paths shared a commitment to innovation, service, and professional readiness. The selected work spans furniture, products, graphic design, packaging, publications, and communication systems, demonstrating that capstone quality is not defined by one scale or medium.

## From Research to Public Work

Students were expected to make, test, and revise throughout the semester. Early research established a consequential problem and criteria for success. Rapid prototypes made assumptions visible. Design development connected form, function, material, fabrication, and use. The final build asked students to resolve their decisions at full scale or a credible level of fidelity.

Communication was treated as part of the design rather than documentation added after the fact. Final deliverables included presentation boards, process books, press releases, photography, identity systems, portfolios, and short films. Students left with both a completed capstone and the material required to explain it to employers, collaborators, clients, and a broader public.

## Course Outcomes

Students were expected to:

- Develop an idea into a fully realized project through research, analysis, creation, and documentation.
- Connect capstone work to personal interests and professional goals.
- Produce clear innovation in service of others.
- Work with accountability across long, self-directed timelines.
- Use critique, mentorship, and peer exchange to strengthen decisions.
- Build and evaluate increasingly resolved prototypes.
- Communicate, promote, and position their work professionally.
- Contribute actively to a collaborative design culture.

Across nine years of teaching the course, the constant was not a particular product category. It was the expectation that students take ownership of meaningful work and learn how to bring it into the world.
