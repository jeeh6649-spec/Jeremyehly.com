# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the syllabus, assignments, student work, and course evaluations. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

Urban Rewilding grew from a question that I believe design education needs to ask more often: who, beyond ourselves, is affected by the environments and objects we make?

The course asked students early in their education to treat non-human species as legitimate design constituents. That required more than adding habitat to a finished proposal. Students had to research how another organism perceives its environment, understand its ecological relationships, and allow those findings to shape decisions about form, material, placement, planting, water, and maintenance.

I was particularly interested in bringing landscape architecture and environmental product design together. The strongest proposals move easily between systems and objects. A rain garden is connected to mosquito control; mosquito control is connected to bat and snake habitat; a nesting structure is connected to planting, seasonal food, predators, domestic animals, and the daily life of a household. Students began to see that design decisions become more meaningful when they are understood as relationships rather than isolated interventions.

This was a new and ambitious eight-week curriculum, and the first offering taught me a great deal about pacing. The research phase was valuable, but several students correctly identified that the final integration needed more time. The course also asked students to manage many interdependent deliverables while working across unfamiliar disciplines. In a future version, I would establish the final project framework earlier, make assessment criteria more explicit, and allow research and design development to overlap rather than treating them as separate phases.

What I would preserve is the intellectual shift visible in the work. Students designed for animals they might normally overlook, fear, or treat as nuisances. They examined conflict as well as coexistence and developed proposals that were practical, speculative, and unusually empathetic. The final projects suggest that ecological literacy can expand design imagination rather than constrain it.
