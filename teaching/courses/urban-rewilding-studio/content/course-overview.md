# Urban Rewilding Studio

**ENVD 1120-211 | Fall 2025 | Eight-week studio**

Designing for a more-than-human city.

Urban Rewilding asked students to reconsider who design serves. As urban development expands and habitat contracts, the studio treated design as a means of negotiating between human occupation and the needs of other species.

Students began by researching candidate species, their ecological roles, vulnerabilities, and perceptual worlds. They then formed interdisciplinary groups, analyzed residential sites, and developed integrated proposals combining landscape architecture with environmental product design. Planting, drainage, ecological connectivity, nesting structures, pollinator habitat, and species-specific infrastructure were designed as parts of one living system.

## Course Structure

+ **Species Evaluation + Empathy** | Individual research established ecological needs and challenged students to think beyond human-centered assumptions.
+ **Umwelt + Interrelations** | Students represented how selected species experience scale, space, threat, and opportunity.
+ **Site Analysis + Site Model** | Groups mapped habitat conditions, barriers, overlaps, drainage, vegetation, and human use.
+ **Final Rewilding Design** | Integrated proposals combined landscape strategies, full-scale product prototypes, fabrication information, and a shared physical site model.

The final work demonstrates ecological reasoning across scales. A habitat element is not presented as an isolated object; it is evaluated through placement, maintenance, material life, relationships with other species, and its contribution to a larger urban ecosystem.

## Learning Through Integration

The studio joined the complementary strengths of landscape and product design. Students had to move between territory and detail, understanding how a planting plan might support a food web while also resolving how a birdhouse, snake refuge, cat perch, or bat pavilion would be fabricated, installed, maintained, and used.

This made the course an exercise in both empathy and synthesis. Students learned to identify ecological consequences, collaborate across disciplinary perspectives, model complex relationships, prototype at multiple scales, and communicate proposals to audiences who may not already see cities as shared habitats.

As a curricular initiative, Urban Rewilding also reflects a deliberate expansion of foundational design education. It asks beginning students to treat ecological interdependence as a primary design condition and to consider, from the outset of their education, who and what their decisions allow to flourish.
