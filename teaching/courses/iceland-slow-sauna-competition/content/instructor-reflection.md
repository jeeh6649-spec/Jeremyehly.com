# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the Spring 2026 syllabus, student work, and course evaluations. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

I chose a small program for this capstone because I wanted students to have nowhere to hide. A sauna and greenhouse can be described quickly, but resolving the relationship among heat, cold, light, material, body, and landscape requires sustained attention. The challenge was not to make more architecture. It was to make each decision carry more weight.

Lake Mývatn gave the studio a demanding context. The volcanic terrain is visually powerful, but I did not want it to become scenery placed behind an object. Students had to decide how architecture would enter the land, what it would reveal or withhold, and how a visitor would move from exposure to enclosure and back again. The strongest projects developed a clear position on that relationship and allowed it to guide the plan, section, material palette, and sequence of experience.

The visits to local wellness spaces were important because they shifted the conversation from images to sensations. Students could notice how long a transition felt, how bodies gathered around heat, how light changed a room, or how cold altered awareness. That kind of direct observation gave them another form of evidence. It reminded us that architecture is not only composed for the eye.

The international competition also raised the stakes in a productive way. The work had to answer a brief, communicate to an audience outside our program, and reach a professional level of resolution by a fixed deadline. I treated drawing, visualization, and layout as part of the design process throughout the semester. Representation was not a final layer; it was one of the ways students tested whether an idea was coherent.

I am proud of the range within the final work. The projects do not repeat one formal solution. They explore dwelling through separate structures, seasonal presence around a shared hearth, a buried progression from darkness to light, and an architecture shaped by basalt formations. What connects them is the effort to make an experience legible from concept through detail.

The student evaluations also identify a change I would make. The research and experiential phase was valuable, but several students felt that it occupied too much of the semester and compressed the time available for design development, graphics, and detailing. I agree that the next version should move into design earlier while preserving the depth of inquiry. I would establish a more compressed research sprint, require its immediate translation into design propositions, and create earlier representation milestones so that graphics develop alongside the architecture.

That reflection does not diminish the value of slowing down; it clarifies what slowness should mean in a studio. It should not mean delaying design. It should mean returning to consequential questions, testing decisions carefully, and resisting progress that is only superficial. My role is to create enough structure for that depth to happen while protecting the time students need to bring ambitious work to completion.
