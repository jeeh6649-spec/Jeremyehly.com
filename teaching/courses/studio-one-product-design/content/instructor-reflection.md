# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the syllabus, assignments, student work, and course evaluations. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

Teaching the first studio in a design sequence carries a particular responsibility. Students are learning skills, but they are also deciding what design is and whether they can imagine themselves becoming designers. I want the course to give them enough structure to make progress while preserving the curiosity, humor, and personal investment that brought them to the field.

Coordinating a course of more than 200 students has made me attentive to the relationship between consistency and belonging. A shared curriculum should give every student a dependable foundation without flattening the distinct perspectives of individual instructors. For me, that means aligning expectations, supporting teaching assistants, and building enough coherence that students can spend their energy learning rather than trying to decode the course around them.

The shift from a self-directed object to a user-based product is central to the course. It asks students to move beyond preference and begin listening carefully to another person. They learn that an interview is not a formality, that a problem statement can change as evidence accumulates, and that a prototype is valuable because of what it reveals rather than how finished it looks.

I am proud of the range visible in the final work. Students addressed remote learning, premature infant care, procrastination, and avalanche awareness through very different products and communication systems. What connects the projects is a willingness to make ideas specific: to draw mechanisms, identify materials, model interactions, test form, and explain why a proposal should exist.

The evaluations reinforce something I value deeply in foundational teaching: students felt that feedback helped them see how their work could improve. They also identified real pressures in the course, particularly workload, coordination with parallel classes, and the length of some desk critiques. Those comments point toward a better balance between ambition and pacing. The goal is not to reduce rigor, but to protect the time students need to think, make, receive useful feedback, and revise with intention.
