# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the Spring 2026 syllabus and course archive. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

The course began with an honest constraint: I could not teach generative AI as settled knowledge. The technology was changing faster than any fixed curriculum could reasonably follow. That instability was not simply a problem to work around; it became part of the premise. Students needed to become comfortable entering unfamiliar territory, testing what was possible, and making informed decisions even when the tools and their implications were still unsettled.

From the beginning, I did not want the course to become a survey of software. Knowing which button to press has a short shelf life. I was more interested in helping students understand where generative tools might enter a design process, what they could contribute, and where they might begin to weaken the designer's own thinking. The practical work was important, but the larger objective was judgment.

That is why many of the workflows began with something intentionally human and specific: a hand-built model, a visual reference, a disciplinary question, or a speculative position. In the control-geometry exercise, students used Duplo constructions to establish form before generating imagery. In the image-to-object workflow, they moved from a directed concept to digital geometry and a physical print. These exercises made it possible to see AI as one participant in a longer process rather than as an automatic source of finished design.

I also wanted the classroom to operate as a shared investigation. My role was to provide a framework, demonstrations, prompts, critique, and encouragement, but I could not pretend to have settled answers about a technology that was evolving in real time. Students brought their own disciplines, instincts, optimism, resistance, and curiosity into the course. Their discoveries during workshops and pinups often shaped the questions we pursued next. That exchange was central to the way the course worked.

The tension between capability and responsibility became increasingly important as the semester progressed. Students could generate and iterate with extraordinary speed, but speed did not necessarily produce meaning. We repeatedly returned to questions of authorship, originality, labor, bias, environmental cost, and creative agency. I wanted students to recognize low-effort or derivative output, but also to identify moments when AI genuinely allowed them to see, test, or make something they could not have reached in the same way before.

The final two assignments were designed as a deliberate pair. For the speculative films, students used the full range of tools from the semester to construct a coherent vision of design and everyday life in 2051. For the personal reflection, they set those tools down. No generative assistance was permitted. After fifteen weeks of prompting, iterating, and collaborating with machines, I wanted to hear the students think in their own voices about what the experience had changed, complicated, or left unresolved.

I am proud that the resulting work does not offer a single uncomplicated story about AI. Some projects are playful and optimistic; others are skeptical, uneasy, or openly critical. The student reflections reveal a similar range. That ambiguity feels appropriate. My goal was never to persuade students that AI was inherently good or bad, but to ensure that they could approach it with enough experience to form a position of their own.

This first iteration also clarified what I would strengthen in the future. The course established a broad practical foundation, but the ethical, environmental, and theoretical questions deserve to be woven even more continuously through the technical work rather than concentrated near the end. As the tools become easier to use, the responsibility of the course shifts further toward helping students decide what is worth making, what forms of knowledge they should protect, and when efficiency is not the most meaningful goal.

For me, responsible AI integration is not about novelty or replacing the creative process. It is about building enough fluency to see these systems clearly: what they make possible, where they fail, and what they ask us to surrender. I want students to leave the course capable of using AI, but more importantly, capable of deciding when and how it deserves a place in their work.
