# Generative Design with AI

**ENVD 4365-002 | Spring 2026 | University of Colorado Boulder**

Generative AI changes more than design output. It changes how designers research, think, make, and communicate.

Generative Design with AI is an advanced environmental design seminar for students in architecture, environmental product design, landscape architecture, and related disciplines. The course combines practical experimentation with sustained questions about authorship, taste, labor, environmental cost, and creative responsibility.

The premise of the course is that generative AI differs from earlier technological shifts in design. These systems do more than increase efficiency or replace physical labor; they participate in cognition, creativity, and decision-making. Learning to use them therefore requires more than technical proficiency. It requires judgment about how, when, and why they should be used.

## A Shared Journey

The course was structured as a journey into territory that was still changing beneath us. The schedule remained intentionally flexible so that new tools, unexpected results, and student curiosity could redirect the work. Lectures and demonstrations established a framework, while workshops, pinups, discussion, and experimentation allowed students' questions and discoveries to shape what followed.

The instructor's role was that of guide and co-navigator: to offer challenges, prompts, technical knowledge, critique, and encouragement while asking students to bring their own disciplines, interests, and skepticism into the room. The goal was not to produce a single approved position on AI, but to build a learning environment in which informed enthusiasm and informed resistance could coexist.

## Learning Through Workflows

The semester began with foundational AI literacy and prompt craft before moving through research, image generation, visualization, video, multimodal tools, and AI-assisted coding. Rather than teaching these platforms as isolated software demonstrations, the course situated them within recognizable design workflows.

Students connected analog models, sketches, prompts, generated imagery, digital geometry, fabrication, and moving images. A Duplo control-geometry exercise demonstrated how a designer could establish intentional formal constraints before working with AI. A prompt-to-image-to-3D-print workflow tested whether generated geometry could become a viable physical object. Weekly design prompts created space for play, failure, repetition, and technical confidence before students were asked to synthesize what they had learned in larger projects.

## Judgment, Agency, and Taste

Technical speed was never treated as the same thing as design quality. Students were asked to compare outputs, identify generic or derivative results, recognize where a tool was weakening an idea, and remain responsible for the decisions that shaped the work. AI could accelerate exploration, but it could not replace the need for a clear point of view.

This emphasis culminated in two complementary final assignments. In one, students set generative tools aside and wrote a personal reflection entirely in their own voices. In the other, they orchestrated text, imagery, motion, sound, and speculative design into short films imagining how AI might transform creativity, professional practice, and the built environment by 2051.

## Course Outcomes

By the end of the course, students were expected to:

- Understand the capabilities and limitations of contemporary generative systems.
- Work fluently across text, image, visualization, video, and emerging multimodal tools.
- Integrate AI into design workflows while retaining control over intent and decision-making.
- Evaluate generated work with discernment rather than accepting output at face value.
- Examine questions of authorship, originality, bias, labor, environmental cost, and intellectual property.
- Use AI for speculative and critical inquiry as well as conventional design production.
- Articulate a personal position on where AI should and should not operate within their practice.

The course ultimately positioned AI literacy as both a practical capability and a critical responsibility. Its aim was not simply to help students make more with new tools, but to help them become more intentional about what they make, what they delegate, and what they choose to keep human.

Student work was exhibited in the ENVD Gallery alongside the AIA traveling exhibition *Architecture After AI*, connecting the course's experiments and ethical questions to a broader disciplinary conversation about design after generative systems.
