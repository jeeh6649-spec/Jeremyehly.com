Graphic Design and Portfolio

ENVD 4052-001|Spring 2025|University of Colorado Boulder
Professional identity as a design problem.

Graphic Design and Portfolio connected foundational visual communication to a practical outcome with lasting value: a coherent professional identity expressed through a PDF portfolio, resume, cover letter, website, and professional network.

The course began with typography, color, composition, hierarchy, image, and visual analysis. Short exercises and poster projects gave students a shared vocabulary for critique and helped them see graphic design as more than surface styling. Those principles were then applied to a longer process of editing, sequencing, clarifying, and presenting work developed across architecture, landscape architecture, planning, and environmental product design studios.

Generative AI was introduced as an editing and development tool, not a substitute for design intention. Students could use AI to clarify writing, test visual directions, or strengthen existing project material, but they were expected to explain how it was used and remain responsible for originality, accuracy, copyright, and the final quality of the work.

My Approach
The Portfolio as a Design Project
I approach the portfolio as a design project in its own right, not simply a container for work completed elsewhere. It asks students to identify an audience, make judgments about what matters, establish hierarchy, write clearly, sequence evidence, and create a visual system that supports the work without overwhelming it. That process is often more revealing than students expect: a portfolio makes gaps visible, showing where a project needs a better diagram, a cleaner image, or a more honest account of the student's contribution.
The graphic-design exercises established a common language before students turned toward their own identities. Poster design was especially useful because it demanded immediate communication and disciplined editing. A viewer must understand that something matters before deciding to look longer, and the same principle applies to a portfolio page, a project opening, and the portfolio as a whole.
The final professional review was the most important test. Practitioners responded not only to graphic quality but to clarity, relevance, confidence, and the student's ability to discuss the work. The portfolio became a bridge between school and the next opportunity, and students left with both a finished artifact and a more precise understanding of how they wanted to be seen.

02 | Exercises
Graphic Design Exercises
Poster projects built a shared vocabulary of type, color, composition, and message before students turned toward their own professional identities.
Post-COVID Public Service Announcements
Students created speculative public-service posters imagining life after widespread COVID immunity. In response to the fear-based imagery that dominated the pandemic's public realm, the exercise used type, color, image, and concise language to communicate anticipation, renewed visibility, collective gathering, and the transition from virtual to physical experience.
