# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the course syllabi, project archive, and published project evidence. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

Design-build has been one of the most consequential parts of my teaching. It places students in a situation where design decisions cannot remain abstract. The work has a client, a budget, a schedule, a site, and people waiting to use it. Every decision carries a real cost, and every unresolved detail eventually appears in the construction.

That reality changes the classroom. Students begin to understand that a strong concept is only the beginning. They must listen carefully, communicate with people outside their discipline, coordinate with one another, document their intent, source materials, adapt to setbacks, and maintain quality through the final day of the build. Their individual ideas matter, but those ideas must become part of a shared project.

My role in these studios is to establish the framework and standards that make ambitious work possible. I guide the client process, structure milestones, teach professional project phases, help students recognize risk, and push the work toward a level of craft that respects both the client and the labor invested by the group. At the same time, students learn an enormous amount from one another. Construction makes knowledge visible and shareable. One student's expertise with modeling, fabrication, lighting, materials, or organization can raise the capacity of the entire studio.

The projects collected here operate at different scales, but they share the same educational premise. The playscape grew from conversations with residents and children in an affordable housing community. The outdoor classroom required students to translate the Denver Botanic Gardens' educational mission into a flexible space delivered within sixteen weeks. The luminous ticket centers asked students to resolve mobility, weather, lighting, interior comfort, branding, and highway constraints within two compact structures.

I am proud of the completed work, but I am equally proud of what it required from the students. They had to move past the point where a rendering could carry the project. They had to make decisions precise enough for someone else to build, and then become the people doing the building. They experienced the pressure and satisfaction of contributing to something larger than their individual portfolios.

Design-build gives students evidence of their own capability. At the end of the semester, the result is not only a proposal or an academic exercise. It is a public project with a continuing life, shaped by their judgment, persistence, and collective effort.
