Advanced Digital Modeling with Rhino

ENVD 2352|Selected teaching, 2010 to 2023|University of Colorado Boulder
Advanced Digital Modeling introduced Rhino as part of a larger design and communication workflow.

Students developed 2D and 3D modeling, photorealistic rendering with V-Ray, advanced surface modeling, parametric modeling with Grasshopper, and integration with Adobe software. The course treated technical ability as a means rather than an end: exercises paired new commands with a design premise, a visual narrative, or a problem worth exploring.

The selected archive spans several offerings of the course. The 2020 Powers of Ten work documents remote teaching during the pandemic, while the earlier parametric pods show the longer development of a design-centered approach to digital instruction.

My Approach
A Reason to Move Past the Tutorial
This course gets at the core of how I teach new technical skills. I do not believe students become more capable designers by completing a sequence of dry software tutorials. They learn when a tool is attached to an idea they care enough to pursue, and when experimentation gives them a reason to move beyond the minimum demonstration.
The duck exercise is intentionally simple: model a duck in Rhino and give it a creative context. That small shift opens the work to humor, puns, narrative, product design, image making, and friendly competition. Students still learn NURBS modeling, but the outcomes make the skill memorable and invite each class to build on the imagination of the last.
The parametric pods demonstrate why I resist teaching software apart from design context. Students had to locate a project, articulate a program, generate form, construct parametric logic, render an atmosphere, and explain the relationship among those decisions. A larger vision gave them a reason to work through technical difficulty.

03 | Exercise
Playful NURBS Modeling
A shared duck model becomes an open invitation to design, composite, render, and make a visual joke.
Duck Context Exercise
A straightforward NURBS modeling tutorial becomes an invitation to design, composite, render, and make a visual joke. The range of outcomes shows students treating a shared base model as an aircraft, product, mascot, advertisement, film poster, and improbable character. Humor creates permission to experiment while repetition builds technical fluency.
