# Instructor Reflection

*Working draft based on the syllabus, assignment briefs, selected work, and teaching notes. This piece will be revised after a reflective interview.*

This course gets at the core of how I teach new technical skills. I do not believe students become more capable designers by completing a sequence of dry software tutorials. They learn when a tool is attached to an idea they care enough to pursue, and when experimentation gives them a reason to move beyond the minimum demonstration.

The duck exercise is intentionally simple: model a duck in Rhino and give it a creative context. That small shift opens the work to humor, puns, narrative, product design, image making, and friendly competition. Students still learn NURBS modeling, but the outcomes make the skill memorable and invite each class to build on the imagination of the last.

The Powers of Ten project applied the same principle at the scale of a semester. Using Charles and Ray Eames' movement across scales as a curricular structure, students progressed from a city to a block, a home, a room, and finally a parametrically modeled virus. In 2020, the pandemic edition gave a remote class a shared narrative through which they could process an extraordinary moment with some levity and hope. I remain proud of the resilience visible in that work.

The parametric pods demonstrate why I resist teaching software apart from design context. Students had to locate a project, articulate a program, generate form, construct parametric logic, render an atmosphere, and explain the relationship among those decisions. A larger vision gave them a reason to work through technical difficulty. Their skills became stronger because the software was in service of something they wanted to make convincing.
